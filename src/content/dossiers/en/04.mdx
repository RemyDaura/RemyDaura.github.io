<Section label="Context" title="784 million people undernourished">

The FAO wants to understand the mechanisms behind global undernourishment to guide its courses of action. In 2017, 9.8% of the world population, nearly 784 million people, was undernourished.

</Section>

<Section label="Need" title="A paradox to explain, figure by figure">

Answer a question that looks paradoxical at first sight: why does hunger persist when world food production is plentiful? This required a solid, fully traceable data foundation, where every figure presented could be justified by the source code.

</Section>

<Section label="Approach" title="Follow the calories, down to the leaks">

Four FAO files were combined with inner joins (`pd.merge`, `how="inner"`), to make sure the analyses only cover countries with all the data required (food availability and population in particular).

The core calculation of the study aggregates the calories available for human consumption: compared with the minimum calorie requirement (2,500 kcal a day), world production could in theory feed 111% of the population, proof that the quantity produced is not the limiting factor.

The analysis then focused on the two major "leaks" in the system: post-harvest losses (before distribution even starts), and the use of cereals for animal feed rather than human food. Boolean indexing and the `.isin()` method made it possible to target these subsets precisely, down to very specific cases such as cassava in Thailand, 80% of which is exported.

To measure the disparities in access to food objectively, countries were compared on their calorie availability per person: some far exceed the recommended needs while others fall below the vital minimum of 2,500 kcal a day.

GDPR compliance: the FAO datasets are aggregated at country level (quantities, populations), and contain no data that could identify an individual.

<Section label="Results" title="Quantity is not what is missing">

<Stats items={frontmatter.stats} />

Three recommendations followed: invest in storage and transport infrastructure to reduce post-harvest losses; reassess the share of cereals used for animal rather than human food, especially in regions under strain; and steer international trade to avoid excessive dependencies (such as the one on Thai cassava) and encourage a fairer redistribution of surpluses.

</Section>

<Section label="Limits and next steps" title="What the country level hides">

- The analysis stays at country level: it does not reveal sub-national disparities (regions, rural and urban areas).
- FAO data has its own estimation methodology, with the uncertainty this brings for some poorly documented countries.
- International trade deserves a deeper look beyond the single case of Thai cassava, to identify other similar structural dependencies.

</Section>
