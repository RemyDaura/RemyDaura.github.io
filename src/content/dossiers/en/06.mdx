<Section label="Context" title="Three siloed sources">

Bottleneck manages 825 products, 714 of them active, "by hand", with data split across silos: stock in the ERP, sales on the website, and the mapping between the two in a separate Excel file. This hand-made management causes copying errors and prevents any clear view of the business.

</Section>

<Section label="Need" title="A single, reliable dataset">

Bring these three sources together into a single, reliable dataset, find the errors that distort revenue and margin calculations, then produce a performance analysis that management can use.

</Section>

<Section label="Approach" title="Reconcile, then find what's wrong">

The first step was methodological: merging the ERP export, the website database and the mapping file into a single dataset. Reconciling them is what brought the truth out: the data was polluted by nine categories of structural errors (negative prices, inconsistent stock, orphan identifiers, duplicates...), not by simple formatting issues.

Three critical anomalies were isolated and presented separately:

- sales recorded at a loss (purchase price higher than the selling price, such as a champagne sold with a -297% margin);
- "ghost" products, active and in stock in the ERP but invisible on the website: revenue lost through a simple synchronisation failure;
- dormant stock, tied up for months.

On performance itself, the Pareto analysis revealed a 60/80 split rather than the classic 20/80: 60% of the catalogue (435 products) generates 80% of revenue. That is a broad "core zone" to secure first, against a long tail of 280 products that brings in only 20% of revenue and ties up most of the dormant stock.

A boxplot was used to check price consistency: the few extreme values found are the house's grands crus and exceptional vintages, a deliberate premium strategy rather than data entry errors. A very weak correlation between price and quantities sold confirms that customers are not driven by low prices and look above all for quality and rarity.

GDPR: the analysis only covers product data (SKU, stock, price) and anonymised transactions. No data that could identify a customer was processed.

<Section label="Results" title="One recommendation per team">

<Stats items={frontmatter.stats} />

Three operational recommendations followed, one per business function:

- **IT**: a lock preventing the ERP from recording a purchase price higher than the selling price, with a data quality indicator (DQ-KPI) tracking the rate of compliant prices;
- **Finance**: separating gross sales from cancellations to calculate reliable net revenue, with an automatic alert above a negative transaction threshold;
- **Logistics**: dual steering, to secure a zero stock-out target on the core zone, and an active clearance policy on the long tail to free up tied-up capital.

</Section>

<Section label="Limits and next steps" title="Reliability over completeness">

- Some data had to be set aside rather than fixed: products with no reliable match between the ERP and the website were excluded from the revenue calculation, choosing reliability over uncertain completeness.
- The proposed recommendations (ERP lock, separate flows, dual steering) prevent the errors from coming back, but do not fix their root cause on the human data entry side.
- This project was the starting point of a later mission (case file no. 13), which automated part of this quality control.

</Section>
