<Section label="Context" title="One grant, three possible areas">

DWFA works on access to drinking water through three areas of expertise: creating services where none exist, modernising existing services, and advising governments.

The organisation has applied for funding. If it is granted, the money will be invested in one of these three areas, in a country yet to be decided.

</Section>

<Section label="Need" title="Inform the decision, without making it">

Give DWFA the data to make this decision with full knowledge of the facts. Not a single recommendation imposed by the dashboard, but a decision tool with three reading levels: global, regional, national.

</Section>

<Section label="Approach" title="Two pitfalls avoided, 21 accessible visuals">

Five source tables, from the WHO and the World Bank:

- access to water, with a key distinction between *basic* access (having water) and *safely managed* access (truly safe water);
- population;
- mortality attributed to unsafe water;
- political stability;
- a reference table linking 194 countries to 6 WHO regions.

Period covered: 2000 to 2017 for water access, up to 2018 for population and political stability.

The preprocessing had to get around two pitfalls. First, the Population table has five rows per country and per year (Total, Urban, Rural, Male, Female): without a systematic filter, the world population gets counted five times. Second, a simple average would give Monaco the same weight as China: every indicator was therefore built as a DAX measure weighted by population.

Before building the dashboard, a **blueprint** set out, for each of the three views, the user need, the measures chosen and the visualisation selected. The data model follows a star schema, with strict one-to-many relationships and DAX measures rather than calculated columns, to stay light in memory.

The specification set accessibility criteria: alt text written for each of the 21 visuals, contrast meeting the WCAG minimum ratio of 4.5:1 (the navy blue used exceeds 9:1), data labels visible without interaction, and a logical tab order.

</Section>

<Section label="Results" title="A water quality problem, not an access problem">

<Stats items={frontmatter.stats} />

The dashboard's central finding: it is not a problem of access to water, but a problem of water quality. In sub-Saharan Africa, basic access falls to 62% and access to safe water to just 7%. The national view goes down to individual countries: Sierra Leone, for example, combines basic access of 60.8%, a mostly rural population and enough political stability to be supported.

Three recommendations followed, one per area of intervention:

- **creating services** in rural sub-Saharan Africa (Chad 38.7%, South Sudan 40.7%, Ethiopia 41.1%);
- **modernisation** in South and South-East Asia, where infrastructure already exists but remains of poor quality (Laos shows 82% basic access but only 16% safe water);
- **government advisory work** in stable countries with high water-related mortality (Sierra Leone, Benin, Comoros).

</Section>

<Section label="Limits and next steps" title="Data that stops in 2017">

- The data stops in 2017: recent changes (crises, investments, conflicts) are not reflected, and these recommendations should be cross-checked against more recent field data.
- The indicators are national; they do not reveal disparities within a country.
- A deliberate choice not to interpolate missing values: the WHO already publishes its data with a built-in estimation methodology, and missing data is often concentrated in the least documented countries, precisely the most fragile ones. Interpolating would have introduced a systematic bias.
- Possible next steps: update as soon as new JMP data is published, create a composite DWFA index, publish on Power BI Service for real-time access in the field.

</Section>
